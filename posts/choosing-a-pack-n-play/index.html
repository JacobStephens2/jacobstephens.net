Choosing a Pack ’n Play

After the baby monitor, I looked at playards, the thing most people call a Pack ’n Play after Graco’s brand name. The use I had in mind: mostly at home as a second baby station outside the nursery, plus car trips to visit family and friends. Friends recommended one with a raised bassinet and a changing table on top, and pointed to a Graco Travel Dome at about $200 and a Baby Trend Trend-E at about $70.

In one line: the raised bassinet + changer combination costs about $90–120, not $200. The Joie Latte Change is the best value on paper if you can find it in stock. The Joie Sesame LX at $180 is the upgrade if you'll fold it often.

“Bassinet attachment” means several things

  • Raised bassinet: an upper sleep level inside the playard, so you aren’t bending to the floor for a newborn. You take it out later and use the bottom.
  • Separate portable bassinet: a smaller unit that lifts out. Nice to have, but you don’t need it just to use the playard in the living room.
  • Changer: a supervised diaper-changing surface. It’s not for naps, and most manuals say to remove it whenever the baby is sleeping below.
  • Seat, napper, lounger: the name doesn’t make it a safe sleep product. Read the manual.

The useful question is what each extra dollar buys: a longer-lasting attachment, an easier fold, easier cleaning, or a second bassinet. Most of the cheap Baby Trend bundles look like bargains until you see that their attachments end at three months. The Latte gets the same equipment with a 25 lb changer for about the same money. If you don’t need a changer at all, a plain raised-bassinet playard for $70–90 may be all you need.

What I’d pay extra for

  • A fold you can do without a fight, if you’ll pack it every weekend. If it stays set up for weeks, this matters much less.
  • Cleaning what actually gets dirty. A washable sheet isn’t a washable mattress pad. Most of these are spot-clean only; the Remi V2 is the exception. Go by the manual, not the marketing.
  • Changer height and support. An awkward changer erases the point of an all-in-one station.

Canopies, toy bars, music, vibration and extra newborn seats rank below those. They add parts to store and carry.

Lighter travel cribs like the Guava Lotus ($250), BabyBjörn Travel Crib Light ($230) and Chicco Alfa Lite Nest ($230) are great for flights, but none include a changer. That makes them the wrong tool for a home care station.

The number on the box is only half the rule

Stop using a bassinet at the earliest of its weight, height, age or developmental limit in your exact model’s manual. Most say 15 lb or when the baby starts pushing up on hands and knees, whichever comes first. A 20 lb rating doesn’t buy more months if the baby starts pushing up first. Never let the baby sleep underneath an installed bassinet.

Safety claims worth ignoring

  • Use the mattress pad that came with it. Don’t add a topper or a “fits most pack n plays” mattress. A wrong-size or thicker pad can create an entrapment and suffocation hazard (CPSC).
  • Keep it bare: baby on their back, a fitted sheet, and nothing else. No pillows, blankets or positioners (CPSC Safe Sleep).
  • “Breathable” doesn’t mean safer. The AAP found no evidence that breathable mattresses reduce SIDS risk.
  • “CPSC approved” isn’t a thing. Manufacturers certify that they meet the play yard rules; the CPSC doesn’t approve individual products.
  • Check hand-me-downs: some older Graco playards came with an inclined-sleeper accessory that was recalled in 2020. Look up the exact model number.

The plan

Compare a $90–120 complete package (Latte if it’s in stock, otherwise Simple Solutions) against the Sesame LX in person. Set each one up, fold it, and test the changer. Measure the room and the trunk. Then buy the exact fitted sheet the manual allows. If visits to one house become frequent, a cheap second playard that stays there beats hauling an expensive one back and forth.
